When Do Cavaliers Get Heart Murmurs? Early Signs Owners Miss

When do Cavaliers get heart murmurs? Learn when mitral valve disease usually appears, which early signs matter, and when to ask for heart screening.

Home›Blog›When Do Cavaliers Get Heart Murmurs? Early Signs Owners Miss

Published: 2026-05-21 · Cavalier King Charles Spaniel Zone

If you own a Cavalier King Charles Spaniel and are wondering when do Cavaliers get heart murmurs, the straightforward answer is that many begin to develop a detectable murmur around 5 to 7 years of age, but changes in the heart valve can start much earlier. This condition, known as myxomatous mitral valve disease (MMVD), is the most common heart problem in the breed, and understanding its timeline is the first step toward protecting your dog's quality of life.

Quick answer: While some Cavaliers may have a murmur as early as 2 to 3 years old, most are diagnosed between ages 5 and 9. By the age of 10, over 90 percent of Cavaliers show some sign of mitral valve disease. The key is catching it before your dog shows symptoms.

What Exactly Is a Heart Murmur in a Cavalier?

A heart murmur is not a disease itself—it is the sound of turbulent blood flow inside the heart. In Cavaliers, the most common cause is a progressive weakening of the mitral valve. This valve sits between the left atrium and the left ventricle. When it fails to close tightly, a small amount of blood leaks backward (regurgitation) each time the heart pumps. Your veterinarian hears this leak as a "whoosh" sound between the normal lub-dub beats.

Think of it like a door hinge that becomes loose over time. At first, the door still closes. It just does not seal perfectly. As the hinge loosens further, the leak gets louder, and the heart must work harder to compensate. This is why the grade of a murmur matters, and why knowing when do Cavaliers get heart murmurs helps you plan the right monitoring schedule.

Mitral valve disease in Cavaliers is inherited, not caused by diet or activity. Almost every affected Cavalier will eventually develop some degree of valve degeneration if they live long enough. This is different from many other breeds, where heart murmurs can be innocent (harmless) or linked to other structural defects. In the Cavalier, a murmur detected during a routine checkup should always be taken seriously.

Why Cavaliers Are Predisposed to Early Murmurs

The Cavalier King Charles Spaniel carries a genetic predisposition for early-onset myxomatous mitral valve disease. Research indicates that the disease progresses faster and appears earlier in this breed compared to mixed-breed dogs or other small breeds like the Miniature Poodle or Dachshund. The reason lies in a specific type of collagen abnormality within the valve tissue itself. The valve gradually thickens, becomes nodular, and loses its ability to form a tight seal.

Several large-scale studies tracking Cavaliers over their lifetimes have produced consistent numbers:

Age Range Percentage of Cavaliers with a Detectable Murmur
2 – 3 years 10 – 15%
4 – 6 years 30 – 50%
7 – 9 years 60 – 80%
10+ years 90 – 95%

These numbers are sobering, but they should not cause panic. Many Cavaliers live happily with a low-grade murmur for years. The goal is not to eliminate the murmur—it is to slow the progression from compensatory to symptomatic heart failure. A Cavalier diagnosed early and managed well can enjoy a normal lifespan with only modest lifestyle adjustments.

It is also important to note that some Cavaliers develop a murmur before they are fully mature. Breeders who screen breeding stock using cardiac auscultation and echocardiography have reduced the incidence in their lines, but no breeding program has eliminated the trait entirely. If you are considering adding a Cavalier to your family, ask the breeder for heart clearances on both parents. Early Signs Owners Often Miss

Understanding when do Cavaliers get heart murmurs is only half the picture. You also need to recognize the subtle ways a Cavalier compensates for a leaking valve. Dogs are masters of hiding discomfort, and Cavaliers in particular are eager to please. They will still wag their tail and ask for walks even when their heart is working overtime. You must learn to see past the happy facade.

The earliest signs are not dramatic. They include the following:

  • Increased respiratory rate during sleep. A normal resting respiratory rate for a Cavalier is between 15 and 30 breaths per minute. Anything consistently above 35 while the dog is sleeping deeply warrants a veterinary check. This is often the very first objective clue.
  • Softer cough. Not a hacking cough like kennel cough. It sounds more like a gentle, single cough or a "honk" after drinking water or first thing in the morning. It may be mistaken for clearing the throat.
  • Reluctance to finish meals. An enlarged heart can press against the stomach, making the dog feel full quickly. Your Cavalier may eat half their bowl and walk away.
  • Pacing or restlessness at night. Dogs with heart enlargement may struggle to find a comfortable position because the heart crowds the lungs.
  • Slightly bluish tint to the tongue after exercise. This is a later sign but is sometimes overlooked in a dog that is simply "tired."

It is vital to establish a baseline for your individual Cavalier. Count sleeping respiratory rates once a week when your dog is in deep sleep. Write it down. This simple habit gives you objective data long before a cough appears. If you notice a trend upward across several weeks, schedule a veterinary appointment even if the dog seems fine otherwise. Do not wait for visible distress. For more on tracking these signs, visit our health section for a monitoring checklist.

When to See the Vet: The Screening Conversation

If your Cavalier is between 3 and 5 years old and has never had a murmur detected, a yearly auscultation (listening with a stethoscope) during the annual exam is sufficient. However, because Cavaliers are high-risk, many veterinary cardiologists recommend a baseline echocardiogram (heart ultrasound) at age 3 or 4, even if no murmur is heard. This gives you a measurement of the valve structure and the left atrial size. If the valve looks normal on ultrasound, you can relax and simply recheck in a year.

When a murmur is detected, the conversation changes. Your veterinarian will grade the murmur from I (very faint) to VI (loud enough to feel with a hand on the chest). The grade is less important than what the ultrasound shows. A grade II murmur may be accompanied by significant valve leakage in some dogs, while a grade III murmur may still have a normal heart size in others. This is why mitral valve disease needs objective imaging rather than sound alone.

Here is a practical rule: if your vet suggests repeating the murmur check in six months, ask directly whether an echocardiogram would be more useful now. The answer depends on whether the dog has symptoms and what the murmur sounds like. If the murmur is new, if it has progressed in grade since the last visit, or if your dog shows any of the early signs listed above, request the echocardiogram sooner. It is a non-invasive procedure that usually requires no sedation and gives definitive information.

You should also request a blood test called NT-proBNP. This biomarker reflects stretch of the heart muscle. A normal result provides strong reassurance that the heart is not yet in distress, even if a murmur is audible. Combining the ultrasound with the NT-proBNP test gives you the clearest picture of where your Cavalier stands on the MMVD timeline. Managing a Cavalier With a Low-Grade Murmur

Once you know when do Cavaliers get heart murmurs and have confirmed your dog's stage, management focuses on slowing progression. For a Cavalier with a low-grade murmur (I or II) and a normal heart size on ultrasound, no medication is needed yet. This is the compensatory phase, where the heart is coping well on its own.

What you should do:

  • Switch to a high-quality, lower-sodium diet. You do not need a prescription diet at this stage, but avoid added salt, processed treats, and human food high in sodium.
  • Maintain lean body weight. Extra body fat forces the heart to work harder. A Cavalier should have a visible waist when viewed from above.
  • Provide moderate daily exercise. Walks are fine. Avoid prolonged sprinting, intense fetch sessions in hot weather, or high-stress activities that cause the dog to pant heavily for extended periods.
  • Consider starting an omega-3 fatty acid supplement (fish oil). There is moderate evidence this supports heart muscle health, though it does not stop the valve degeneration itself.
  • Monitor sleeping respiratory rates weekly. Many owners use a simple smartphone timer and log the number in a note.

It is also the right time to discuss dental health with your veterinarian. Dental disease creates chronic low-grade inflammation that can stress the cardiovascular system. Cavaliers are prone to dental issues, and a professional cleaning under anesthesia, with appropriate safety precautions, is generally safer for the heart than leaving infected gums untreated. Your vet will determine the right timing based on the murmur grade. For details on dental care, refer to our dental guide.

If your Cavalier's murmur progresses to grade III or higher, or if imaging shows an enlarged atrium, your veterinarian will likely start medication. The current standard of care involves pimobendan, a drug that improves the heart's pumping efficiency and delays the onset of congestive heart failure. This is not a treatment you start prematurely; starting pimobendan too early, before the heart has enlarged, provides no benefit. Comparing Murmur Grades and What They Mean for Daily Life

To help you understand how a murmur grade translates into daily management, here is a practical breakdown of what each grade typically looks like in a Cavalier. This table works alongside your veterinarian's ultrasound findings.

Murmur Grade Typical Finding on Ultrasound Daily Lifestyle Impact
I (Very faint) Normal or very minimal leakage No restrictions. Normal exercise and diet.
II (Soft) Mild leakage, normal heart size Moderate exercise, low-sodium diet, monitor breathing.
III (Moderate) Moderate leakage, possible early enlargement Reduced high-intensity exercise. Start omega-3s. Discuss medication.
IV – VI (Loud) Significant leakage, enlarged atrium Medication likely needed. Avoid stress. Emergency signs require immediate vet.

This table is not a replacement for professional imaging, but it gives you a framework for conversations with your veterinarian. A grade II murmur in a Cavalier under seven years old is a good time to begin proactive monitoring and dietary changes. What Emergency Signs Look Like

No article about when do Cavaliers get heart murmurs is complete without a clear description of urgent situations. Mitral valve disease progresses in stages, and the transition from compensated to decompensated heart failure can happen over days or even hours. You need to recognize when a veterinary emergency room is the right place.

Go to the emergency vet immediately if your Cavalier shows any of these signs:

  • Labored breathing that does not improve within a few minutes of rest. The abdomen moves in and out visibly with each breath, and the dog may extend the neck to breathe.
  • Gums that are pale, grey, or bluish. Normal Cavalier gums should be bubblegum pink.
  • Collapse or sudden weakness, especially after exercise or during excitement. The dog may cry out, fall over, and then recover within seconds. This is a syncopal episode and requires urgent evaluation.
  • Distended belly combined with a cough and rapid breathing. This suggests fluid accumulation in the abdomen.
  • Refusal to move, stand, or eat for more than a few hours accompanied by noisy breathing.

Do not wait for a scheduled appointment if these symptoms appear. The emergency team can stabilize your Cavalier with oxygen, injectable diuretics, and supportive care. Many dogs return home within 24 to 48 hours and resume a good quality of life for months or years, provided the owner remains vigilant afterward. Always call the emergency clinic on the way so they are prepared for a suspected heart failure patient. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a Cavalier have a heart murmur and live a normal lifespan?

Yes, many Cavaliers diagnosed with a low-grade murmur in mid-life go on to live 12 to 14 years with appropriate monitoring and lifestyle management. The key is early detection and regular veterinary follow-up.

What age do Cavaliers typically get heart murmurs?

Most Cavaliers develop a detectable murmur between 5 and 7 years of age, though a small percentage can have one as early as 2 to 3 years old. By age 10, the vast majority have some valve changes.

Is a heart murmur painful for a Cavalier?

A murmur itself is not painful. However, the progression to congestive heart failure can cause discomfort from fluid buildup in the lungs and difficulty breathing. Early stage dogs are comfortable and active.

How much does a heart ultrasound cost for a Cavalier?

Costs vary regionally, but a complete echocardiogram by a veterinary cardiologist typically ranges from $400 to $700. Some clinics offer screening packages with a reduced fee. This is considered an essential diagnostic test for the breed.

Should I breed a Cavalier that has a heart murmur?

No. Since MMVD has a strong genetic component, breeding a Cavalier with a known murmur increases the likelihood of earlier onset and faster progression in offspring. Reputable breeders screen breeding stock with echocardiograms and select clear dogs.

Does exercise make a heart murmur worse in a Cavalier?

Exercise does not cause the murmur to worsen, but it can unmask symptoms if the heart is already struggling. Moderate, consistent exercise is beneficial. High-intensity or prolonged exertion should be avoided once a significant murmur is present.

Can a Cavalier's heart murmur go away on its own?

No. Myxomatous mitral valve disease is progressive and does not reverse. The murmur may remain stable for years, but it will not disappear without treatment. Some innocent murmurs in puppies can resolve, but these are rare in Cavaliers and should still be investigated.
